# Housing in East Detroit: Two governments, overlapping work
## Home Forward, Detroit, and the basis for East Detroit's housing institutions

**Municipal housing research | 10 November 2026**

### Housing institutions serving East Detroit

East Detroit residents are served by two housing institutions with separate governing bodies, finances and program responsibilities.

1. **City and County of East Detroit Housing Division (EDHD).** Part of the consolidated municipal government, accountable through the city-county executive, council, appropriations, and public records process. The division sets and administers local housing policy and programs: housing development and preservation finance, local tenant and homeowner assistance, homelessness prevention, and coordination with planning and human services. It may manage locally financed housing or contracts under municipal authority. It cannot allocate an independent authority's federal vouchers.
2. **East Detroit Regional Housing Authority (EDRHA).** An **independent public housing authority**, with a separate governing commission, management, budgets, federal housing agreements, property and rental-assistance operations, and accountability duties. Its service territory includes East Detroit and **participating surrounding communities beyond the city-county boundary**, making its constituency broader than the city. Its regional structure resembles Home Forward's jurisdiction in Oregon. It administers federal housing assistance and its housing portfolio, with local or regional programs where agreements authorize them. The city-county cannot direct its board or seize its records merely because the authority serves city residents.

The distinction is between two public bodies with separate mandates **within a consolidated city-county**, not between two competing governments called "the city" and "the county." The overlapping residents and programs create genuine administrative problems. Both institutions operate in 2030. Coordination requires agreements on records, funding, staffing and who responds when a resident needs help.

## 1. What the real Detroit model actually contains

### Detroit Housing and Revitalization Department (HRD): a city department

Detroit's **Housing and Revitalization Department** is an office of city government. Detroit says the department directs housing policy, manages HUD entitlement funds and municipal housing investments, finances new housing and preservation, supports homeowners and renters, and collaborates with partners on homelessness. The city's own description specifically names its partnership with the **independent Detroit Housing Commission**. HRD is the clearest precedent for an East Detroit municipal housing division. [D1] [D2]

Detroit's published 2025–2030 Affordable Housing Strategy sets targets of **preserving 10,000 affordable units and constructing 3,000** over five years. These are Detroit policy targets, not achieved results or population-scaled assumptions for East Detroit. They show a municipal department choosing investments and measuring production and preservation independently of a voucher authority's daily operation. [D3]

### Detroit Housing Commission (DHC): separate authority and federal relationship

The **Detroit Housing Commission** operates as a public housing authority, administering rental assistance and housing. It is governed separately from city HRD. After administrative, financial, and management failures, HUD took control of DHC's operations on **July 7, 2005**. HUD announced a transition plan in **January 2012** that required sustainable governance, an oversight agreement, and performance improvements. DHC states HUD released it from the recovery administrator on **March 13, 2015**. Under Michigan law, a **five-member board appointed by Detroit's mayor** governs the commission, including at least one subsidized-housing resident. Mayoral appointment does not make DHC another city department. [D4] [D5] [D6]

This history demonstrates that a housing authority can be **locally governed and federally accountable at the same time**. The city can influence board appointments and coordinate policy; HUD can impose federal program rules and intervene when the agency fails to meet its obligations.

### Detroit's operating problems were concrete

- **Income and voucher recertification:** Outlier Media reported that understaffing produced long backlogs in the federal voucher program. In April 2023, **221 of 1,960 due recertifications** had been completed; in 2024 reporting, a backlog of **1,747 delinquent recertifications** remained despite outside contractor support. Rent amounts and subsidies can be wrong while a recertification is pending. Landlords reported delayed payments and some stopped accepting DHC vouchers. [D7] [D8]
- **Capacity and unmet demand:** Outlier reported that the agency was using roughly **1,500 fewer vouchers** than its capacity in one period, partly because it lacked staff to process required work. A housing benefit authorized on paper is different from a household actually receiving and using it. [D8]
- **Vacancy and condition:** In a 2023 Outlier interview, the interviewer put public housing occupancy at about **78%**. Later reporting described vacancy near **23–24% in September 2024**, along with problems with rent collection and deteriorating buildings. [D9] [D10]
- **Recovery with limitations:** In a 2025 follow-up, Outlier reported that DHC management said it had mostly cleared the recertification backlog, vacancy had fallen from **24% to 14%**, and **all 15 complexes** had passed that year's HUD inspections. The article also reported residents whose individual housing problems persisted. These improvements were reported outcomes and management assertions in their stated period, not proof that every resident issue was resolved. [D11]

**What Detroit illustrates:** A shared digital form cannot resolve unstaffed queues, inaccurate payment calculations, inspections, landlord distrust, or an underfunded property portfolio by itself. Agency staffing, authority, incentives, and financing are part of the service.

## 2. What Home Forward actually is

**Home Forward** is a public corporation operating as Portland and Multnomah County's housing authority, and it also serves Gresham and communities in east Multnomah County. It explicitly states that it is **not part of the City of Portland's government structure**. It works with the city, county, and other partners and also administers some programs on their behalf. It runs housing assistance, affordable properties, and related services. [H1]

### Board, jurisdiction, and accountability

Home Forward has a **nine-member volunteer commission**: **four Portland representatives, two Gresham representatives, two Multnomah County representatives, and one housing-program participant**. The board establishes policy, approves the budget, and hires the executive director. The appointing governments influence who serves without directly administering every Home Forward housing transaction. [H2]

Portland also has a **Portland Housing Bureau** that finances affordable housing, supports preservation, and administers local housing policies. Portland therefore provides a *second* actual example of a city housing bureau working alongside a separate housing authority. The East Detroit arrangement combines this pattern with Detroit's HRD/DHC division of responsibilities. [H3]

### Federal flexibility: Moving to Work

Home Forward joined HUD's **Moving to Work (MTW)** demonstration on **April 1, 1999**. MTW gives selected public housing authorities limited flexibility to design local approaches to funding and program administration, subject to federal conditions and reporting. It does not give the city unrestricted access to federal records or guarantee additional housing supply or sound agency management. In October 2026, Home Forward was reportedly considering a change in MTW participation partly to address voucher funding. [H4] [H5]

### A documented operational and financial crisis

These are **different evidence points**, not one undifferentiated allegation:

| When | Reported finding | What to examine |
|---|---|---|
| **November–December 2025** | A November vacancy report described **956 of 6,847 units vacant**, about **14%**. Later coverage rounded the count to 955. The agency cited damaged units, market conditions and other causes; a large vacancy count does **not** imply every unit was ready for immediate occupancy. [H6] | Unit condition, affordability, waiting lists, location, eligibility, and leasing throughput |
| **February 2026** | Data supplied by Home Forward showed an average of **185 days to fill a vacancy in 2025**. Directly managed buildings averaged **172 days**; other management contractors had differing averages. [H7] | Who owns each handoff, property management contracts, and measures of time actually available to lease |
| **February–March 2026** | Reporting identified high vacancy, missed rent revenue, deteriorating debt-service coverage, tenant safety concerns, and inconsistent performance among contracted property managers. A March report described a manager averaging **292 days** to fill units. [H8] [H9] | Oversight of contractors; financial feedback loops; resident safety |
| **April–May 2026** | Willamette Week reported **over $100,000 of agency-funded CEO travel during 2023–2025**. Home Forward announced on **April 29** that CEO Ivory Mathews would resign effective **May 1**; Michael Buonocore became interim chief. Officials and residents contested the board's oversight. [H10] [H11] | Board scrutiny, conflicts, executive reporting, leadership and public accountability |
| **August 2026** | The Oregon Government Ethics Commission voted to **investigate** the former CEO's travel spending. The vote started a fuller investigation; it was **not a finding of misconduct**. [H12] | Distinguish accusations, preliminary grounds, and formal findings |
| **October 26, 2026** | Home Forward's interim director estimated a **$38 million 2027 budget gap** and said the **2026 shortfall** had also risen from an earlier **$13.8 million estimate to nearly $38 million**. A possible **$28 million HUD shortfall payment for 2026 was not guaranteed**. Measures under discussion included staff reductions, reduced administrative spending, changes to tenant rent shares, and reconsidering MTW terms. The proposed tenant contribution changes were expected to affect **12,356 households**. [H5] | Forecasting, federal dependence, cost shifts to tenants, and authority to change benefits |

**The central tension:** A housing authority can face intense unmet housing need while simultaneously carrying vacant apartments, slow leasing, high operating costs, a strained budget, and tenants reporting serious living-condition problems. No single statistic identifies the cause. The vacancy figure includes properties requiring work, differing affordability restrictions, and units whose rents may have become uncompetitive with some market offerings. The right question is *which operational, financial, legal, or housing-market conditions produce each delay or vacancy?*

**The governance tension:** A separately governed authority can have public representation and formal controls while still experiencing serious oversight failures. MTW-style flexibility cannot replace accurate financial reporting, staffing, property performance, or a functioning board.

## 3. Side-by-side: two precedents for two East Detroit institutions

| Question | Detroit city department + DHC | Portland Housing Bureau + Home Forward | East Detroit starting condition |
|---|---|---|---|
| Municipal housing office | Detroit HRD sets policy, invests funds, supports production/preservation and homelessness services [D1] | Portland Housing Bureau finances housing and local protections [H3] | **EDHD**, a required city-county department |
| Separate authority | DHC has its own federally accountable programs and mayor-appointed five-member board [D6] | Home Forward is an independent public corporation with nine commissioners drawn from multiple jurisdictions [H1] [H2] | **EDRHA**, independent regional authority |
| Constituency | Detroit city plus federal and development partners | Portland, Gresham, Multnomah County and other county communities | East Detroit **and participating neighboring communities** |
| Distinct sources of money | City investment/entitlement funds versus authority housing assistance/property finance | Local housing funding versus federal vouchers/property revenue/other sources | Separate city and authority funds, with terms for joint projects |
| Failure highlighted | Recertification backlogs, staffing, occupancy, inspection and property issues [D8] [D11] | Vacancy turnover, debt pressure, forecast gap, governance and oversight [H7] [H5] | Assess service times, staffing, budgets and property conditions |
| Possible point of cooperation | Development, preservation, homelessness referrals and resident service | Housing finance, shared projects, landlord and property operations | Defined intake, referrals, local assistance, housing placement, record correction, financial coordination |
| Reason they may disagree | Independent duties and federal voucher administration | Regional constituency, separate board, funding restrictions | EDRHA may refuse a requested integration or seek contractual conditions |

**The research question is not whether one city/county should “own housing.”** It is whether two agencies sharing a population have compatible goals and legal capacities, and whether removing duplication preserves the functions that must remain separate.

### What overlap can and cannot tell us

**Potential savings to investigate:** duplicated household intake, repeated address verification, conflicting program directories, staff referrals without case tracking, duplicative landlord requests, inconsistent vacancy reporting, and multiple support desks without a clear handoff.

**Distinct work that should not be silently merged:** program-specific federal eligibility and appeals; HUD voucher funding; property ownership and debt; inspections and habitability; city appropriations; regional board accountability; and resident confidentiality.

**Possible adverse effect of a shared process:** more households are referred to a program that has no funding, staff, available unit, or landlord ready to accept them. Faster eligibility screening might enlarge a queue if the limiting step is supply or leasing, not intake.

## 4. The actual housing transaction and where responsibility sits

A household is behind on rent and faces losing its home. The example shows how the two agencies might serve one household.

| Step | City Housing Division could do | Regional Housing Authority could do | Other participants |
|---|---|---|---|
| Understand need | Provide a city-funded prevention program, navigation or referral | Identify an existing voucher/lease/portfolio relationship where authorized | Household, landlord, advocates |
| Check place and identity | Use municipal address or residency service under adopted rules | Use its own records and HUD verification rules for its programs | GIS, county human services, HUD |
| Evaluate program rules | Decide eligibility for local funds | Decide eligibility, rent calculations, and voucher/lease rules for its separate programs | State or federal program administrators |
| Determine available assistance | Allocate a finite city-funded grant | Confirm actual voucher budget, available property, subsidy and inspection requirements | Finance, property manager, landlord |
| Deliver a result | Pay a qualifying landlord or issue a local decision/referral | Issue/adjust assistance or offer a qualifying unit subject to program terms | Resident, landlord, courts where relevant |
| Correct errors and resolve complaints | Correct the local case and permit appeal under city program rules | Correct its official voucher/tenancy record and use required review process | Original record holder and any independent appeal body |

A proposed shared application, verified-status exchange, assisted referral, or resident credential is an **implementation choice**. It requires exact agreement on who sends what, for which purpose, under which rules, and who corrects a wrong result. Each agency must decide whether it can rely on the other's verification.

### Federal constraints relevant to the older “one-click housing” idea

HUD already operates the **Enterprise Income Verification (EIV)** system, using income and benefit information obtained through authorized federal arrangements. Public housing authorities use EIV under program rules; it is **not** a general municipal tax/payroll registry available for city-wide benefits. See HUD's system description and **24 CFR § 5.233**. [F1] [F2]

For Housing Choice Vouchers, federally required verification and reexamination mean an income flag alone does not establish that a funded voucher or suitable unit exists. A city may finance a separate locally governed rent-relief program, but cannot manufacture additional federal vouchers by changing an interface. [F1] [F2] [F3]

The older proposal's automatic court stay should also be withdrawn as a claimed city power. Eviction court rules, notice, case status, landlord action, and judicial authority need an actual governing basis; a housing authority's automated referral cannot itself cancel a legal proceeding.

The useful ambition survives: **stop making households repeatedly find and re-prove the same facts when lawful and reliable processes could reduce that burden**. The means, scope, financing, and division of authority remain decisions.

## 5. Coordination questions

The City Housing Division and Regional Housing Authority serve many of the same households. Each retains its own budget, records, program rules, appeals and governing body.

Areas for joint planning include applications, referrals, housing placement, development and preservation, income verification, rent assistance, landlord engagement, complaints, financial reporting and resident support. A workable agreement must identify which agency provides each service and how to respond if the other declines participation.
