## 3. Neighborhood descriptions and competing claims

### South / Historic

**Old Watkins (ED01).** Legacy village, small businesses, agricultural families, aging owners and renters. The population is 10,158, with 38% renter households and a $103,200 median household income. 

**Watkins Junction (ED02).** Rail-adjacent mixed housing, service workers and newly arrived families. The population is 20,908, with 66% renter households and a $71,600 median household income. 

**Colfax East (ED03).** Older commercial corridor, lower-cost apartments and frequent moves. The population is 18,289, with 73% renter households and a $58,900 median household income. 

**South Yards (ED04).** Industrial jobs, truck access, worksite housing and pollution concerns. The population is 12,271, with 77% renter households and a $62,100 median household income. 

### Central / Downtown

**Civic Square (ED05).** Civic center, mixed-income midrise homes and public-service demand. The population is 26,418, with 62% renter households and a $92,400 median household income. 

**Central Station (ED06).** Major transfer station, dense rental housing and night economy. The population is 28,405, with 75% renter households and a $89,700 median household income. 

**Foundry Quarter (ED07).** Converted production sites, offices and relatively affordable family flats. The population is 25,070, with 71% renter households and a $74,500 median household income. 

**Eastbank (ED08).** Riverside greenways, apartments, growing professional population. The population is 21,115, with 47% renter households and a $119,300 median household income. 

### West / Residential

**Inboden (ED09).** Older subdivided homes, ownership concentration and school access. The population is 15,507, with 33% renter households and a $126,400 median household income. 

**Quail Run (ED10).** Townhomes, duplexes and childcare shortages. The population is 15,651, with 46% renter households and a $95,600 median household income. 

**West Commons (ED11).** Family apartments, multifamily infill and school crowding. The population is 22,520, with 52% renter households and a $84,700 median household income. 

**Cavanaugh (ED12).** Transit-adjacent apartments and wage-dependent households. The population is 16,726, with 60% renter households and a $78,100 median household income. 

### East / Growth

**Manila (ED13).** Large mixed-use blocks, newer homes and rapid infrastructure demands. The population is 24,790, with 39% renter households and a $112,600 median household income. 

**Schumaker (ED14).** Working households near advanced logistics and long commutes. The population is 17,407, with 45% renter households and a $97,700 median household income. 

**Harback (ED15).** Small fringe settlements, wells, septic conversion and annexation disputes. The population is 11,442, with 53% renter households and a $86,500 median household income. 

### North / Expansion

**Prairie Crossing (ED16).** New housing, high school enrollment and transit expansion debates. The population is 22,663, with 43% renter households and a $107,900 median household income. 

**Northfield (ED17).** New ownership-focused development with uneven nearby service access. The population is 21,787, with 29% renter households and a $134,800 median household income. 

**Box Elder (ED18).** Flood-prone drainage crossings, renters and seasonal workers. The population is 18,676, with 69% renter households and a $66,800 median household income. 

**Portside (ED19).** Employment district edges, shift work and airport-related noise. The population is 13,690, with 62% renter households and a $76,700 median household income. 

**High Prairie (ED20).** High-income new housing, large lots and infrastructure contributions. The population is 18,924, with 26% renter households and a $151,200 median household income. 

## 4. What residents are dealing with



1. **Housing costs and instability.** Old homeowners and newer renters have sharply different financial positions. South Yards and Colfax East have high renter concentrations and severe cost burdens. The city has not settled emergency rent assistance, tenant information, inclusionary housing, or legal support.
2. **Two levels of address and identity.** Older farm addresses, newly subdivided parcels, and apartment complexes can be mismatched between county GIS, postal delivery, tax rolls, utility accounts and school assignment systems. An account is not itself proof of residency.
3. **Water and land conversion.** A region using groundwater, agricultural parcels, special districts, and utility extensions cannot assume an unlimited supply. Households, housing developers, and industrial employers compete for service commitments. The actual CASP plan describes regional water/wastewater needs.
4. **Travel across large distances.** Much of the city-county is low-density land. The urban center has a rail hub, but areas north and east have limited frequency, late-shift workers, and unresolved transit participation. RTD cannot be ordered to operate a municipal route.
5. **Employment without matched housing.** Logistics, manufacturing, entertainment, medical institutions, schools and services create varied shift schedules, pay levels, noise, and labor demand. Not every household benefits from aerospace investment.
6. **Public authority and appeals.** The county administers state-supervised benefits under Colorado's state-supervised model. The city-county must decide how new city services interact with those programs without taking over state-level eligibility rules.
7. **Health and emergency access.** The city has a trauma center. Decisions about dispatch integration, patient information, E911, emergency access, public safety and hospital data are unsettled.
8. **Schools and young families.** Rapidly changing enrollment, new attendance boundaries, and school transport create urgent demands. School districts are independent; their participation is negotiable.
9. **Growth pressure on agricultural and floodplain land.** Existing farmland, drainage corridors and protected airspace remain real constraints. Urbanization produces value and displacement risks for landowners, tenants, farmworkers and nearby communities.
10. **Participation and representation.** Neighborhoods vary in language access, income, tenure, commute and ability to attend evening meetings. A citywide digital vote or app may reproduce those differences rather than resolve them.

## 5. Six household service cases

| Case | Household and situation | Question for agencies |
|---|---|---|
| H01 | Two adults, one child, renters in Colfax East; one hourly worker's schedule changes weekly | Can they complete and correct an application without taking time off work? |
| H02 | Retired homeowner in Old Watkins; parcel has an older rural address and an unmatched new GIS record | Which office owns correction, and who keeps a pending request open? |
| H03 | Three-generation household in Box Elder; Spanish is the preferred service language | What is available in person, by phone, and in translation? |
| H04 | Transit-dependent shift worker in Portside; employer has a private shuttle that ends before the final shift | What happens if RTD or employer declines a service partnership? |
| H05 | Single parent in West Commons with two children entering different schools | Can city services coordinate without claiming control over an independent school district? |
| H06 | Small business owner in South Yards whose address and parcel records do not match | Who decides whether a permit proceeds while county GIS corrects the source record? |

The class can add cases, change details and argue about their meaning. Every tested service should have at least one normal case, one disputed-record case, one outside-partner refusal, and one case requiring human assistance or appeal.
