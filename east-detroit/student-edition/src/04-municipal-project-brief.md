# Your assignment

You play a department head, agency leader, civic representative or partner in the fictional City and County of East Detroit, Colorado, in 2030. The city has 382,417 residents and twenty provisional neighborhoods across a large, unevenly developed territory. It has major needs in housing, water, transit, benefits, address records, schools, safety and community facilities.

Your class writes the specification (spec) for a city-county version of Estonia's digital government. In Estonia, each agency keeps its own records and exchanges data through a shared, secure layer called X-tee, under agreements between the organizations (see Reading 2 and the Directory). The spec sets out how East Detroit's departments and outside agencies could do the same:

- which records they hold
- what they share, and on what terms
- who runs the exchange
- how residents are protected
- which services come first

Nothing is approved yet: no digital identity, app, procurement, vendor, cloud platform, data exchange, governance board or first service. Your job is to help establish the facts, develop the spec and its implementation plan, and negotiate with other agencies to carry it out.

# Choose your entry

You own one entry: a city-county department, an outside agency or a resident group. Find it in the **Directory of Departments and Agencies** and claim it on the **Sign-up Sheet**. The instructor plays every entry nobody claims.

| Group | Entries | Status |
|---|---|---|
| City-county departments | D-01 to D-15 | Must take part |
| Outside agencies | A-01 to A-08: school district, library district, water and sewer authority, RTD, State human services and IT, Regional Housing Authority, County Court, Health and Hospital Authority | Always represented; may refuse to join |
| Optional outside agency | A-09: East Detroit Health System | Takes part only if a student claims it |

Outside agencies are worth claiming: one that refuses can stop a service.

City-county departments take part because East Detroit assigns them. If you own one, you can still contest policy, details, budgets, feasibility, legal authority and sequence.

# What to bring to the first session

- Your entry and its service problem, with one specific resident case
- The records your entry holds that others need, and what it needs from others
- The decision you propose, and the real or fictional authority it depends on
- A second option and its trade-off
- At least one internal dependency, and one outside organization that might accept, negotiate or refuse
- What you expect to see if your plan works, including a failure or exception case

Label your sources: mark real facts as **REAL REFERENCE**, facts created for the course as **SCENARIO**, and unresolved choices as **OPEN**. Do not present fictional demographics, new ZIP codes, governance powers or data agreements as existing Colorado law.

# What is settled and what is open

## Settled for the exercise (SCENARIO)

1. East Detroit exists as a consolidated city-county coping with rapid growth.
2. Its residents and services are diverse. The demographics and neighborhoods in Reading 1 are planning assumptions you can question. They are not real Census estimates.
3. The city-county has operating city and county departments and elected leaders. The City Housing Division and the independent East Detroit Regional Housing Authority both exist.
4. Its civic and cultural anchors are the ones in Reading 1: The Hangar at Space Center, the Denver Fawns, the Colorado Apex, the Denver Pilots at Aerotropolis Park, EDPAC and the East Detroit Health System.
5. Some bodies are independent of the city-county: the school district, library district, water and sewer authority, RTD, the County Court, the two hospitals, the Regional Housing Authority, state and federal agencies, airport operators and other jurisdictions.

## Open for the class (OPEN)

- The exact county boundary and the neighborhood boundaries
- The local charter and how residents are represented
- Which services to deliver first, and how
- Water financing
- Partnerships with outside agencies
- How information is handled and shared
- Technology and procurement
- The form, delegated powers and priority rules of any shared service, including any agreement between the two housing institutions
- Funding, ownership and digital systems for the civic and cultural anchors

## Ideas from earlier drafts, open for debate

Earlier drafts presented these as adopted. None was. You can debate, change or reject each one:

- ED-Core digital identity and ED-Wallet
- "Once only" as a fixed municipal law
- Universal zero-knowledge YES/NO data APIs
- Automated housing approvals and income checks
- Automatic school enrollment triggers or seats
- RTD fare integration
- Mandatory telemetry for parks access
- A 48-hour limit for provisional GIS records
- 60-minute emergency access tokens
- Automatic historic preservation holds
- Digital voting on public art
- Digital onboarding through libraries
- Vendor procurement and cloud hosting choices
- A phased platform launch
- A specific board size and membership

Earlier drafts also implied 100% voucher uptake, uniform student assignment and "zero burden" outcomes, with no implementation evidence behind them.

# Three project stages

## Stage A: Establish the basis

Find out the city-county's duties, needs, existing assets, costs, resident cases and limits of authority. Identify priorities and dependencies. You may challenge the city's starting assumptions.

**What you do:**

- Name the community need or service problem your role sees.
- Identify the resident or worker who faces it, and the neighborhoods where it matters.
- Separate what East Detroit already provides in the scenario, what another agency controls, and what is unknown.
- State the authority, money, staff, information, facilities and partners your role needs.

## Stage B: Develop an implementation plan

Choose service priorities, decision-makers, governance or procurement options, first working demonstrations, financing, staffing, technical and data dependencies, review routes and sequence. During a shared build, the instructor turns agreed decisions into a draft implementation spec.

**What you do:**

- Propose what to do first, and why.
- State the policy decision, who can authorize it, how the work runs, how it is paid for, what must be built or bought, and what users should see.
- Name an alternative you considered, and how you would test the result.
- Choose the level of technology and partnership you can justify.

## Stage C: Coordinate stakeholders

Outside agencies may accept, negotiate or refuse. If one says no, redesign without it. Saying no still counts as taking part.

**What you do:**

- Identify which other departments or outside organizations must take part.
- Request their consent or an agreement.
- If they decline, record their reason and revise the plan.
- If they join only on conditions, record the conditions in the decision record.
- Make sure someone is responsible for complaints, corrections, appeals and ending an arrangement that is not working.

## What the class produces

- A baseline register
- Named owners for each stakeholder relationship
- A map of services and who has authority over them
- Proposed charter provisions or agreements where needed
- A log of decisions and dissents
- An implementation plan
- One or more demonstrations or tests of how a service behaves

# Four governance decisions

**Authority.** Which public body can approve a shared service, adopt standards, spend public money, invite a partner and stop a harmful practice? Is it an existing city-county function, a new authority, an intergovernmental agreement, a nonprofit, or a combination? Who can overrule whom?

**Ownership and operations.** Who controls the domain name, cloud or hosting accounts, source code, contracts, records and any analysis of them? Who answers users, keeps service hours, patches systems, handles complaints and takes over if a vendor fails? Separate each asset and each responsibility.

**Information.** For every data exchange, identify the sender, receiver, purpose, fields, legal or fictional authority, consent or notice, how long data is kept, who corrects it, who reviews it, how incidents are handled, and how optional sharing can be revoked. "Once only," ED-Wallet, zero-knowledge APIs and automated referrals are options, not policy.

**Money and incentives.** Compare general appropriations, agency contributions, user fees, developer fees, land-value mechanisms, grants, special districts and contract revenue. Explain who pays, who benefits, and what happens if expected revenue does not arrive.

# Choosing the first service

The course does not pick the first service. You choose it through evidence and negotiation. Candidates include:

- Correcting addresses across new parcels
- Permits
- Emergency housing and tenant assistance
- Intake for county-administered food or cash programs
- Transit access
- Recreation access
- Connections to schools
- Library and community support
- Emergency routing
- Public art or venue services

The first launch can be one complete, useful transaction, several connected touchpoints or a staff-only process, if the class can justify it.

**For each candidate service, ask:**

- Which law or simulated authority controls the decision?
- Which agency handles complaints, review and appeals?
- What happens if the other agency is offline, declines to take part, or supplies a wrong record?
- What test would reveal a harmful design, including for a resident who has no phone or whose address is disputed?

# Negotiating with independent agencies

A strong partner agreement states:

- Scope and legal basis
- The parties
- Service levels
- Data fields and permitted uses
- Who corrects records
- How incidents are handled
- Payment
- Limits on using data for training or resale
- Public transparency
- Audit rights
- How either party can revoke or exit
- Duties during a transition

A memorandum of understanding (MOU) is enough when it only coordinates intentions. Enforceable duties need the right legal instrument. Record negotiations in the class decision record.

**Example: connecting to schools.** The city wants to offer a school enrollment referral to residents who register a move. The school district could: agree to a secure address-check service; agree only to provide fixed referral information; demand funding and manual review; or refuse. City GIS can still keep a municipal address record. It cannot make the school's binding enrollment decision without the district.

**Example: regional transit.** RTD might offer a timetable or fare exchange, accept a limited pilot, or decline. The city can still publish route information or fund a separate, legally supported mobility program. A rail hub in the scenario does not require RTD to connect its ticketing systems.

# Questions your entry must answer

1. Which problem will your entry own, which residents does it affect, and in which neighborhoods?
2. What do the city-county, the state (through county administration) and other public bodies deliver now? Which powers does East Detroit have, and which sit elsewhere?
3. Who can approve policy, allocate resources, authorize access, correct records, hear disputes and stop a harmful launch?
4. Which partners are indispensable, what deal must be struck before launch, and what happens if a partner refuses to join?
5. What will residents, frontline staff, reviewers and elected officials see when the service runs, both when a case succeeds and when it fails?
6. How will you know whether the chosen arrangement works better, and whom might it leave out?

# What happens in class

You present your proposals and challenge each other's assumptions. A department can bring several proposals. The class compares conflicting requirements and decides what to test.

The instructor then uses AI, in front of the class, to build or revise a working implementation model. It might be a service diagram, a screen, a data exchange, a staff work queue, a cost model or a test of an agreement. Every choice in the model can be traced to who proposed it.

An AI-generated screen or workflow shows only that an arrangement can be simulated. It does not mean its rules or funding are adopted.

The record keeps who proposed what, what the group chose, who dissented and what changed when the class tried it. Record failures too, and keep earlier versions so the class can return to them. You do not need unanimous agreement. Record disagreements, refusals and missing information. Where a decision is still contested, test more than one path.
